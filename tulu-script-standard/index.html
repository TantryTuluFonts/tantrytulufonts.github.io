A standard for the Tulu script

Tuḷu has a long written tradition and well over a million speakers, yet its traditional script has never had a standard orthography. Tuḷu Varṇāmr̥ta proposes one. We hold that it should be adopted and maintained.

The problem

Tuḷu script usage varies across texts, regions and periods. A learner who meets three different ways of writing the same sound has no way to proceed unless someone says which one to use. Existing resources either assume you already know a related script, lack any real prescriptive clarity, or wander into language learning that goes well beyond the script. None of them fix the inconsistency at its root.

The same gap affects everyone who works with the script today. Digital fonts, Unicode support and teaching materials all involve orthographic decisions, whether or not anyone admits it. Without a written standard, those decisions are made quietly, differently, and often without reasons.

What the standard is

Tuḷu Varṇāmr̥ta sets out one coherent, rule-governed orthography for the traditional Tuḷu script and applies it without exception. It is built on three commitments:

  1. The traditional script, exactly as it is. No simplification, no invented forms, no unnecessary revision. Where existing practice was already systematic, the standard adopts it as it is.
  2. One rule, one answer. Where practice diverged or fell silent, the standard proposes a resolution and explains the reasoning, instead of leaving the learner with a menu of alternatives.
  3. Room for modern Tuḷu. The standard accommodates what the language needs today, including words borrowed from English and other modern languages.

How it works

The standard rests on reasons that can be checked. A few examples:

  • Letters are classified by shape. Each consonant letter is either a śirovarṇa (head-letter) or a pucchavarṇa (tail-letter), according to where its terminal stroke ends and in what direction. That single classification explains how the u and ū signs attach, how conjuncts form, how signs behave on conjuncts, and which virāma a letter takes.
  • The virāma follows the geometry. A pucchavarṇa takes the sūryavirāma and a śirovarṇa takes the chandravirāma, so there is a rule where there used to be habit.
  • The gemination mark has one job. It is reserved for onomatopoeic and expressive reduplications. Every other doubled consonant uses the standard conjunct.
  • Loanwords are handled layer by layer. Each sound has a defined way of being written, from Sanskrit borrowings through to English, so a modern word is not a special case.
  • Every letter is paired with IAST. The romanization pins down exactly which sound and which orthographic decision each script form stands for, which makes the standard something you can check, not just trust.

Why it should be maintained

A standard only works if it is kept. When books, fonts, keyboards, software and classrooms all follow the same rules, their work can be read, checked and combined. Departures from the standard become visible as departures, not as proof that no standard exists.

The standard also protects the script itself. The Foreword to the book puts the position plainly: the traditional Tuḷu script should be preserved in its original form, taught to coming generations, and promoted, without simplification or change. A shared standard is how that is done in practice, so that "the traditional script" means the same thing to every writer.

It is our firm position that the script that has come to us through tradition should be preserved in its original form, taught to coming generations, and promoted.

Śrī Śrī Vidyēśatīrtha Svāmiḷ, Pīṭhādhipati, Śrī Bhaṇḍārakēri Maṭha, Bārkūru, in the Foreword to Tuḷu Varṇāmr̥ta (translated from the Kannada)

The reference fonts

A standard needs fonts that follow it faithfully. Mallige (ASCII) and Mallige Unicode are the faithful representations of this standard, and the script examples in the book are set in Mallige. The same work is being extended to the other fonts in the family, and they are being ported to Unicode too. The one exception is Allige, which was forked from Mallige and stays as it is.

Open to argument

The standard is meant to be examined, argued with and improved, not taken on faith. Every decision in the book is explained, so a reader who disagrees can see exactly where, and why. That is how a standard stays alive: by being used, tested and kept consistent.

Who should adopt it

  • Teachers and learners of the script, who need one set of rules to teach and to learn.
  • Writers and publishers setting Tuḷu in the traditional script.
  • Font designers and software developers deciding how the script should behave on screen. The script examples in the book are set in Mallige and typed with the AccTantra key map, both published here and free to use.
  • Researchers who need a stable reference for reading and citing Tuḷu material.

Read the standard

The full standard, with every rule, its reasoning and its examples, is in Tuḷu Varṇāmr̥ta: A Prescriptive Primer to the Tuḷu Script by Prahlad Prasad Tantry.
